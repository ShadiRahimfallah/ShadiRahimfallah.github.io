Job Market Paper

Financial Frictions, Used Capital, and Misallocation

Paper (PDF)

Renting second-hand equipment ties up less collateral than renting new, so credit-constrained firms can operate closer to their efficient scale. In the benchmark model, calibrated to Vietnamese firms, access to used capital removes 39% of the misallocation that collateral constraints create and repairs 23% of the entry they block among productive low-wealth entrepreneurs.

Presented at

  • Macro Development Group (MDG), York University, 2026

Earlier version — Financial Frictions, Used Capital, and Firm Dynamics — presented at

  • 59th Annual Meetings of the Canadian Economics Association (CEA), Université du Québec à Montréal, May 2025
  • Macro Development Group (MDG), York University, 2025
Abstract

Standard macro-finance models treat capital as homogeneous. Financially constrained firms can therefore adjust only the quantity of capital they operate, not its vintage. This assumption is at odds with the data: young, small, and credit-excluded firms use second-hand equipment disproportionately. I build a dynamic macro-finance model in which firms rent both new and used capital. Second-hand capital lets constrained firms operate closer to their efficient scale, even when they cannot meet the collateral requirements for new capital. It thereby reduces the dispersion of marginal revenue products of capital and weakens the dependence of entrepreneurship on initial wealth. I calibrate the model to Vietnamese firms and find that used capital mitigates the allocative and occupational distortions created by financial frictions.

Publication

Geoeconomics of Global Energy Transformation

Farkhondeh Jabalameli and Shadi Rahimfallah · Journal of World Sociopolitical Studies, 7(1), 55–87, January 2023 · Article · DOI

Abstract

Using monthly data from January 2013 to December 2017 and an autoregressive distributed lag model, this paper examines the relationship between WTI crude-oil prices and U.S. high-density polyethylene prices during the shale-gas expansion. The results identify a positive and significant long-run effect of oil prices on polyethylene prices, no significant short-run effect, and one-way causality from oil to polyethylene prices. The findings explain how inexpensive shale-based ethane, combined with polyethylene prices that remained connected to oil-market conditions, contributed to the competitive advantage of U.S. petrochemical producers.

Master's Thesis

Shale Gas and the Export Competitiveness of Iran's Petrochemical Industry

Master's thesis, University of Tehran, 2019 · Supervisor: Farkhondeh Jabalameli

Overview & findings

This thesis studies how the U.S. shale-gas revolution changed global petrochemical competitiveness and what it means for Iran's petrochemical industry. It combines a time-series analysis of WTI crude-oil and U.S. polyethylene prices, a panel analysis of polyethylene prices and the stock-market performance of major Iranian petrochemical companies, a company-level study of Shazand Petrochemical Company, and an institutional analysis of Iran's feedstock-pricing system. Competitiveness depends on much more than access to natural gas: feedstock costs, international product prices, exchange rates, technology, capacity utilization, and access to export markets all matter, and sanctions raise banking, insurance, and shipping costs and concentrate exports in a few markets, mainly China. The policy conclusion is that cheap feedstock alone cannot create a lasting advantage. Iran needs a transparent, predictable feedstock-pricing formula that balances a fair public return with regional competitiveness (for example, combining domestic opportunity cost with regional competitor prices, smoothed with a moving average and a price collar), together with effective export-credit support and more diverse export destinations.

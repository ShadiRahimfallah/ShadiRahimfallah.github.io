Capacity Market Design for Large New Loads with Jeremy Lin (Johns Hopkins; formerly PJM)Working Draft • Electricity Markets / IO

Data-centre growth is pushing up the capacity prices that every customer in PJM pays. This project designs a market-based approach for integrating large new loads into PJM's capacity market while protecting existing customers from cost shifts.

The first draft formulates the design as linear programs, proves its main cost-allocation and pricing properties, and tests it in a PJM-wide model of the 2030/31 delivery year built from public PJM and Market Monitor data, comparing price caps and treatments of bring-your-own generation.

Second-Hand Machines, Firm Capability, and Technology AdoptionWorking Paper • Empirical IO / DevelopmentSlides
Micro foundation (the firm’s decision)
  • Firm Data: Capability, not wealth, predicts using only used capital once size is held fixed, and all-used firms are not short of capital (World Bank FAT survey; Vietnam SME panel).
  • Firm Decision: Firms choose new or used capital. New capital embodies a better technology, but using it requires an up-front cost that falls with capability (know-how) and must be financed under a collateral constraint (money).
  • Result: Firms using only used capital are 96% capability-bound and 4% finance-bound.
Macro implications (the economy)
  • Adoption: Raising capability lifts the new-technology share from 93.1% to 97.3%. Removing the credit constraint lifts it only to 93.3%, so capability has 17 times the effect.
  • Output: Removing the constraint raises output by 1.6%, mostly through more capital rather than better technology. Raising capability adds 0.15%.
  • Misallocation: Finance distorts how much capital firms use, while capability determines which technology they use.
  • Policy: Training and information about technology raise adoption far more than cheaper credit. Banning used machines raises costs for capability-bound firms without raising the technology they can use.
IO extension (the used-machine market)
  • Buyers and sellers of used machines meet through search, and capability lowers search costs because capable firms know which machine to buy and how to judge it. The used price is set in the market, so policies change prices as well as choices.

Many firms in developing countries produce only with second-hand machines. Is it because they cannot pay for new machines, or because they cannot use the technology new machines carry? Using the World Bank FAT survey (15 countries) and a panel of Vietnamese small and medium manufacturers, I show that capability strongly predicts using only second-hand machines, wealth does not once firm size is held fixed, and these firms are not short of capital. A calibrated model of new versus used capital, with an adoption cost that falls with capability and a collateral constraint, attributes 96% of these firms to a lack of know-how and 4% to finance.

Where Canada’s Energy Goes: Fuels, Electricity and End Uses, 2024–2025

Summary

Which fuels does Canada use, how much is turned into electricity, where does the energy end up, and how much is lost along the way? Using Statistics Canada’s official energy balance, I map these flows as an interactive Sankey diagram for Canada and each region, for 2024 and 2025.

In 2025, fossil fuels supplied 77% of the energy used by industry, transport, farms, homes and businesses, and electricity supplied 23%. Oil products supplied 89% of transportation energy. Fuel-burning power plants turned only about 38% of their fuel into electricity, losing 856 petajoules as heat, and Canada exported 59% of the primary energy it produced. The provinces differ sharply: electricity supplies 88% of residential energy in Quebec, while Alberta’s power plants run almost entirely on natural gas and lose more energy than they deliver. The report includes region and year selectors, key indicators and the full table of energy use by fuel and sector.

Powering Data Centres: PJM, U.S. Markets and Canada’s Provinces

Summary

Data-centre requests now arrive faster than firm power and transmission can be built. Who builds the new supply, who pays for it, and how fast can it arrive? This economic and policy briefing compares how PJM, the other U.S. electricity markets and Canada’s provinces answer these questions, and shows that each answer follows from how the market is designed.

PJM’s capacity price has hit its $325/MW-day cap in three straight auctions, and its 2028/29 auction cleared 6.8 GW short of the reliability requirement. Alberta’s data-centre requests equal about 153% of its record peak, and Ontario’s pipeline about 40% of its peak. BC instead caps data-centre allocations at 400 MW over two years, and Québec proposes a rate about twice the industrial rate. The report includes interactive maps of every market, a comparison of summer and winter capacity markets, a calculator for how much new demand data centres could add, Ontario’s virtual power plant, a timeline of short-run and long-run technology options, and a policy toolkit. All figures come from system operators, utilities and governments.

How Canadian Firms Work: Evidence from the 2024 Enterprise Survey

Summary

What holds Canadian businesses back, who innovates and exports, how firms finance themselves, and how this differs by firm size, sector, region and gender. Using firm-level survey data, I map the main obstacles facing Canadian firms and run 35 applied analyses on innovation, productivity, finance, labour, trade, infrastructure, regulation and ownership.

The report has four parts: an overview of the survey, descriptive findings on obstacles, innovation, trade, finance, gender, regions and green practices, thirty-five further analyses (including exporter productivity premia, the R&D–innovation–productivity chain, credit constraints, gender gaps, tax-compliance burden by firm size and a business-environment index), and a data and methods section. All estimates use survey weights and design-based standard errors. The firm-level data are licensed by the World Bank and are not reproduced; the report shows aggregate results only.

Canada’s Exporters and the U.S. Market, 2000–2025

Summary

How dependent are Canadian exporters on the United States, and which firms and provinces are most exposed? Using a quarter-century of Statistics Canada data on every goods-exporting business, I show that the share of exporters selling only to the U.S. fell for a decade and has risen again since 2020, and that small firms are the most dependent.

About 85% of exporting enterprises sold to the United States in 2025, and about 65% sold only there, up from a low of 56% in 2010. Small and medium-sized enterprises make up 97% of exporters but ship 41% of export value. Three in four exporters sell to a single country, while the top 100 exporters account for about 60% of goods exports. Every chart in the report includes the underlying data and a CSV download.

One Dollar, Thirteen Economies: Input–Output Multipliers Across Canada’s Provinces, 2010–2022

Summary

When an industry in a Canadian province produces one more dollar of output, how much of that dollar stays in the province, how much flows to other provinces, and how much leaves Canada as imports? Using Statistics Canada’s provincial input–output multipliers, I trace each cent through the supply chain, map the results, test whether neighbouring provinces look alike, and group the provinces into four types of economy.

Ontario and Quebec lose the least to other provinces, 5.7¢ and 7.3¢ of each dollar, but Ontario sends 19.6¢ abroad. Nunavut and the Northwest Territories send about 23¢ of each dollar to the rest of Canada, and this spillover is the only measure that clusters geographically (Moran’s I = 0.31). Alberta, Saskatchewan and Newfoundland and Labrador keep 76–78¢ at home but support only about six jobs per $1 million of output, against nearly ten in Prince Edward Island and Nova Scotia. New Brunswick sends 28.6¢ abroad, the most of any province, likely because of its oil refinery. The report includes an interactive map, trends from 2010 to 2022, rankings of the industries in every province, and the cleaned data as CSV files.

Jobs, Wages and Training in Vietnam’s Small Manufacturers, 2007–2015

Summary

How did jobs in Vietnam’s small manufacturing firms change as the economy grew? Using five waves of a worker survey (6,878 interviews in 1,219 firms), I track wages and gender gaps, contracts and social insurance, how workers find jobs, who gets trained and whether training pays, and how job quality differs across provinces.

The median real monthly wage rose 43% between 2007 and 2015, while women earned 86–91% of the wages of comparable men throughout. The share of workers with a formal labour contract rose from 48% to 54%, and employer-paid unemployment insurance from 28% in 2011 to 41% in 2015, almost entirely among contract holders. Seven in ten workers found their job through family or friends, and fewer than one in ten through an agency or job advertisement. Trained workers earn about 11% more than untrained workers, but the premium falls to 6% when they are compared with co-workers in the same firm. The report includes a map of job quality across the ten survey provinces.

Master's thesis

Shale Gas and the Export Competitiveness of Iran's Petrochemical Industry

University of Tehran, 2019
Abstract

This thesis studies how the U.S. shale-gas revolution changed global petrochemical competitiveness and what it means for Iran's petrochemical industry. It combines a time-series analysis of WTI crude-oil and U.S. polyethylene prices, a panel analysis of polyethylene prices and the stock-market performance of major Iranian petrochemical companies, a company-level study of Shazand Petrochemical Company, and an institutional analysis of Iran's feedstock-pricing system. Competitiveness depends on much more than access to natural gas: feedstock costs, international product prices, exchange rates, technology, capacity utilization, and access to export markets all matter, and sanctions raise banking, insurance, and shipping costs and concentrate exports in a few markets, mainly China. The policy conclusion is that cheap feedstock alone cannot create a lasting advantage. Iran needs a transparent, predictable feedstock-pricing formula that balances a fair public return with regional competitiveness (for example, combining domestic opportunity cost with regional competitor prices, smoothed with a moving average and a price collar), together with effective export-credit support and more diverse export destinations.

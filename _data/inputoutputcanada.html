One dollar,
thirteen economies

When an industry in a Canadian province sells one more dollar of output, that dollar is split up along its supply chain. Part of it becomes wages and profits in the same province, part of it buys inputs from other provinces, and part of it pays for imports from outside Canada. Statistics Canada’s input–output multipliers let us follow each cent.

What the numbers show

Big economies make most of their own inputs

Ontario and Quebec lose the least to other provinces: 5.7¢ and 7.3¢ of each dollar. Ontario still sends 19.6¢ abroad, second only to New Brunswick, largely because its manufacturing runs on imported parts. For Ontario car makers, 68.6¢ of every dollar of output pays for imports somewhere in the supply chain.

The North is supplied from the south

Nunavut and the Northwest Territories send about 23¢ of every dollar to other provinces, four times Ontario’s share. Most goods and many services are brought in from southern Canada, so a large part of each northern dollar becomes income somewhere else.

Resource economies keep value, not jobs

Alberta, Saskatchewan and Newfoundland and Labrador keep 76–78¢ of each dollar at home, near the top of the country. Yet they support only 6.0–6.5 jobs per $1 million of output, against 9.7–9.9 in Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island. Oil, gas and potash use a lot of capital and comparatively few workers.

New Brunswick imports its crude

New Brunswick sends 28.6¢ of every dollar outside Canada, the most of any province. The likely reason is its large oil refinery: 84.2¢ of each dollar of refinery output goes to imports, which for a refinery means mainly crude oil.

Do neighbours look alike?

Moran’s I tests whether provinces that share a border have similar values. Positive values mean neighbours resemble each other, and values near −0.08 (what random placement would give) mean there is no pattern. Neighbours share a land border; Prince Edward Island is linked to New Brunswick by the Confederation Bridge and to Nova Scotia by ferry. With only 13 regions, treat the p-values as signals rather than proof.

Only one measure clusters clearly: the share that spills to other provinces (I = 0.31, p = 0.05). The three territories sit side by side and all rely on southern suppliers. Jobs per $1 million show a weaker pattern (I = 0.23, p = 0.10), with a low-jobs block running from Alberta and Saskatchewan into the Northwest Territories and Nunavut. Import leakage has no spatial pattern: it depends on each province’s industries, not on where it is.

Four kinds of provincial economy

I described each province with seven measures for 2022: kept at home, spent in other provinces, spent abroad, jobs per $1 million, labour’s share of GDP, the share of the GDP multiplier that comes from household spending, and the output multiplier. After standardizing them, k-means found that four groups fit best (silhouette score 0.48), and Ward’s hierarchical clustering produced exactly the same four groups. The chart places provinces on the first two principal components, which together hold 72% of the variation.

How things changed, 2010–2022

Pick a measure and a province to highlight. The grey lines are the other twelve.

  • Newfoundland and Labrador now keeps 77.5¢ of each dollar, up from 70.3¢ in 2010, as its import share fell from 18.2¢ to 12.8¢.
  • New Brunswick’s import share moves with world oil prices: 29.9¢ in 2013, 21.6¢ in 2020 and back to 28.6¢ in 2022.
  • Saskatchewan’s share kept at home jumped from 71.8¢ to 76.3¢ in 2022, the year of the commodity price boom.

How this was made

The data

Statistics Canada table 36-10-0595-01, Input-output multipliers, provincial and territorial, detail level: 4,998,743 rows covering 13 provinces and territories, about 220 industries, 14 variables, 7 multiplier types and two geographic coverages, for 2010 to 2022. I kept the “Total industries” rows for every year and every industry for 2022. No values are missing or flagged. Canadian territorial enclaves abroad (embassies and bases) are left out.

What a multiplier measures

Every multiplier is per $1 of an industry’s output. Direct effects happen in the industry itself, indirect effects in its suppliers and their suppliers, and induced effects come from households spending the extra wages. Simple = direct + indirect; total = simple + induced; type II = total ÷ direct. “Within province” counts only effects inside the province; “all provinces” adds the effects in the rest of Canada.

From multipliers to cents

With simple multipliers, each dollar of output is eventually paid out as value added, imports or taxes on products:

GDP at basic prices + international imports + taxes on products − subsidies on products = $1

In this table the sum falls between 99.1¢ and 100¢ for all 169 province-years. Kept at home is the within-province GDP multiplier; other provinces is the all-provinces GDP multiplier minus the within-province one; outside Canada is the all-provinces imports multiplier.

Spatial join and statistics

Boundaries come from Natural Earth (1:50m, public domain), projected to Statistics Canada’s Lambert conformal conic projection and simplified with mapshaper, then joined to the table on DGUID (2021A0002 plus the two-digit province code). Spatial weights use shared land borders (rook contiguity) plus Prince Edward Island’s two fixed links, row-standardized. Moran’s I p-values come from 20,000 random permutations (one-sided).

Clustering

Seven 2022 measures, standardized. k-means with 50 random starts for k = 2 to 6 gave silhouette scores of 0.38, 0.42, 0.48, 0.36 and 0.30, so k = 4. Ward’s hierarchical clustering gives identical groups. Principal components are used only to draw the chart.

Limits

Input–output multipliers assume fixed production recipes, no capacity limits and constant prices. They describe average past relationships, not forecasts, and a specific project can differ a lot from the “Total industries” average. The table shows how much spills to other provinces in total, not which province receives it. Thirteen regions is a very small sample for spatial statistics. Jobs are per million current dollars.

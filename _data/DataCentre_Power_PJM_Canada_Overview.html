Powering data centres: who builds the power, who pays, and how fast?

A guide to how the US electricity markets, with a focus on PJM, and Canada's provinces are dealing with the data-centre wave. Click the map: PJM opens the PJM page, and a province opens the Canada page.

1 · The bottleneck is time, not energy.Data centres want power in 18–24 months. A new gas turbine ordered today arrives around 2031, and transmission takes 5–10 years.
2 · The real fight is over who carries the risk.Consumers (PJM's capacity price), the developer (Alberta and Texas), or the province through rationing (BC, Québec). Market design decides.
3 · Flexibility is the cheapest MW.Every data centre has backup power. Written into a contract, it lets the site connect sooner and protects other customers.

PJM & US markets

PJM: the price signal hit a political ceiling

PJM runs the grid for 13 states plus DC, from Chicago to Virginia. It is home to "Data Center Alley" in Loudoun County. Its capacity market went from among the cheapest in North America to price-capped in three auctions, and data centres are the main reason.

How PJM's capacity market works

1 · Forecast

PJM forecasts peak load 3 years out and sets a reliability requirement: enough capacity for a loss-of-load event no more than once in 10 years.

2 · Auction

Plants, batteries and demand response offer MW of "accredited" capacity. A sloped demand curve sets one price in $/MW-day. Accreditation discounts each MW for how reliable it is in risky hours.

3 · Pay and perform

Winners get paid for the year and face penalties if they fail to perform in emergencies. Consumers pay through their utility bills.

What happened

  • The price jumped from $28.92 (2024/25) to $269.92 (2025/26), then hit the cap three times.
  • Supply could not respond within 3 years because of turbine backlogs and the interconnection queue.
  • Higher prices became a transfer from consumers to existing plants.

Policy response

  • Price collar ($175–$325) extended to 2029/30.
  • Reliability Backstop Procurement: a one-time buy of new capacity, with costs aimed at utilities serving data centres. FERC accepted it on Sep 29, 2026.
  • Large loads without their own supply can be curtailed first.

Benefits vs challenges

  • + Deep market. Prices reveal scarcity.
  • + Backstop puts new-supply cost on data-centre utilities.
  • − Gas turbines are sold out to around 2031.
  • − Local moratoria are spreading across PJM states.
Economist's reading: a scarcity price cannot build plants faster than supply chains allow. PJM is quietly adding long-term contracts on top of its market, which is close to Ontario's model.

Summer vs winter: does PJM have seasonal capacity markets?

Not yet. PJM buys one annual product. Since 2028/29 it pairs summer-only and winter-only resources into one commitment, and a full summer/winter market is in design because winter demand is growing faster than summer. Other markets are already further along:

How big is the ask? My own estimate

Each province's data-centre demand set against the size of its own grid, using operator figures. The categories differ (requests, caps, plans), and that difference is the policy story.

Reading the chart

  • Alberta faces requests larger than its whole grid. That is why it made developers bring their own generation.
  • Ontario sits in the middle (~40%), enough to justify a ministerial gate (Bill 40) and a separate rate class.
  • BC and Québec look small only because policy has capped them.
Economist's reading: markets reveal demand and then cap prices. Crown utilities hide demand by capping quantity. Either way, someone is rationing.

Ontario in detail

The IESO plans 24 years ahead. Bill 40 (Dec 2025) gave the Minister the final say on data-centre connections, and the draft Playbook (Aug 2026) sets the tests: economic value, sovereignty, community benefit and environment.

IESO outlook (Nov 2026)

  • Capacity covered to 2033. Gaps of 950 MW in 2035 and ~2,500 MW from 2036.
  • Energy gaps of 8.5 TWh in 2032 and 20+ TWh from 2036.

Rate design

  • Proposed Class C rate for new data centres over 1 MW.
  • They would be excluded from ICI (the peak-shaving discount).
  • The risk: that also removes the incentive to cut load at peak.

On the ground (panel)

  • Grandbridge: 80 MW connected in 18 months.
  • Enova: 10 MW fits existing feeders. 150 MW needs a new station.
  • NPEI: certainty of load, beneficiary pays, and confidence to invest.

Ontario's virtual power plant and data-centre flexibility (IESO)

A virtual power plant (VPP) links thousands of small devices (smart thermostats, batteries, EV chargers) and dispatches them together like one power plant. At the conference the IESO argued that data centres could join this kind of flexibility.

Peak Perks: Canada's largest VPP

  • The IESO designed and launched it in about six months.
  • It has 320,000+ households and businesses and delivers ≥220 MW when called. In 2025 it averaged about 211 MW per event.
  • Since May 2026 the IESO publishes demand-side activations on its website, so the resource is visible and verified.

The data-centre angle (IESO panel)

  • Every data centre has a UPS with ~2 hours of backup. A 2,000 MW campus therefore holds something like a 2,000 MW / 2-hour battery.
  • Demand response, ICI and peak tools are about 25% of Ontario's resources. If data centres offered 20–25% flexible load, connecting them would get much easier.
  • The Class C rate proposal removes ICI for new data centres. A flexibility credit would keep the incentive to cut load at peak.

+ Benefits

  • The fastest "new MW": months, not years
  • No new plant, wires or fuel
  • Lowers peak cost for everyone

− Challenges

  • Only counts if it is contracted, metered and penalized for non-performance
  • Limited duration (hours, not days) in long cold snaps
  • Backup on diesel or gas adds local emissions

Policy idea

Offer data centres a faster connection or lower rate in exchange for a VPP-style commitment: X MW cut within Y minutes, up to Z hours a year, verified by the IESO. That turns backup power into a capacity resource.

Recommendations

1 · Price the cost caused

Charge new large loads the incremental cost of supply and wires, and recycle any surplus to households (as Manitoba's levy does).

2 · "Flexible now, firm later"

Offer fast curtailable connections backed by on-site backup, with a path to firm service as supply arrives.

3 · Clean the queue

Escalating deposits and milestones. Phantom load leads to over-building.

4 · Go seasonal

Winter risk is rising. Separate summer and winter capacity products, as Ontario, MISO and NYISO have, value resources correctly.

5 · Rules before discretion

Publish size thresholds, scoring and timelines. Investors can price a rule, not discretion.

6 · Municipality first

Social licence is now on the critical path. Engage early and lead with community and Indigenous equity.
